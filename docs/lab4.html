Linear classifiers and gradient descent

Three simulations from the lab, rebuilt to run in the browser. Each one follows the ritual used in class: predict first, move the slider, then compare.

Demo 1

Place the boundary by hand

A linear classifier computes a score z = w₀x₀ + w₁x₁ + b and predicts the sign of z. The 100 flowers are iris setosa (y = −1) and versicolor (y = +1), described by standardised petal length and width.

Predict first. If you double the scale of w (the two weights and the bias together), which numbers in the table change: the error rate, the four losses, or both?

Compare with the answer

Only the losses change. Multiplying (w₀, w₁, b) by 2 multiplies every score by 2 and leaves its sign alone: the boundary z = 0 does not move, so the error rate is the same. The four losses read the margin itself, which doubles: this is why minimising a loss also chooses a scale, and why the lab adds the term λ/2 ‖w‖² to control it.

Demo 2

One flower, four losses

Every loss of the lab is a function of a single number, the margin m = y·z: positive when the flower is on the right side, larger when it is far from the boundary. Its slope ℓ′(m) is the force with which that flower pulls on w, since the gradient for one flower is ℓ′(m)·y·x.

Predict first. A flower is very well classified, at m = 2.5. Which of the four losses still pushes w, and in which direction?
LossFormula ℓ(m)LossSlope ℓ′(m)

Compare with the answer

Linear regression is the only one with a positive slope at m = 2.5 (slope m − 1 = 1.5): it pulls the boundary back towards a flower that is already correct, because it wants every margin to equal exactly 1. The perceptron and the SVM have slope 0 there, and the logistic slope is negative but nearly 0 (about −0.08).

Demo 3

Gradient descent, one iteration at a time

The objective is L(w) = mean of ℓ(mᵢ) + λ/2 ‖w‖². To draw it as a map, this demo keeps two weights (w₀, w₁) and no bias. Each iteration applies the same rule, w ← w − η ∇L(w), starting from w = (−3, 3), the starting point used in the lab.

Predict first. With linear regression, raise the step η from 0.2 to 1.5, then to 1.9. Does the path reach the minimum faster, or does something else happen?

A larger step first helps, then the path overshoots the valley and zigzags across it. Past a threshold the iterates move away from the minimum at every step and the objective grows without bound: the descent diverges. For a quadratic objective the threshold is 2 divided by the largest curvature, which is why the lab asks for the step to be tuned and not simply made large.
